# Commodity Market: Data That Drive Prices and Assets (From Macro → Micro)

Commodity markets are different from stocks, forex, and crypto because they are fundamentally **physical markets**.

The core question behind commodity pricing is:

> **How much physical supply exists today, how much demand exists today and tomorrow, and how difficult is it to move/store/replace that commodity?**

Commodity markets include:

* **Energy:** crude oil, natural gas, gasoline, coal
* **Metals:** gold, silver, copper, aluminum, lithium, iron ore
* **Agriculture:** wheat, corn, soybeans, coffee, cocoa, sugar, cotton
* **Livestock:** cattle, pork

The World Bank and IMF maintain global commodity databases covering energy, metals, agriculture, fertilizers and commodity price dynamics. ([Banque Mondiale][1])

# LEVEL 8 — Futures Market Data

Most commodity trading happens through futures.

Key data:

## Futures Curve

Analyze:

* Contango
* Backwardation

### Contango

Future price > current price

Meaning:

* abundant supply
* storage available

### Backwardation

Current price > future price

Meaning:

* immediate shortage
* strong demand

## What the best firms actually achieve

The world's leading quantitative firms—such as Renaissance Technologies, Citadel, Two Sigma, Jane Street, and XTX Markets—do not aim to be right all the time.

Instead, they optimize:

* Positive expected return (positive expectancy)
* Risk-adjusted returns
* Robustness across changing market regimes
* Capital preservation
* Continuous adaptation

Many profitable quantitative strategies have prediction accuracies between **50% and 60%**, yet remain highly profitable because their average winning trade is significantly larger than their average losing trade.

For example:

* Win rate: **54%**
* Average winner: **+3.2%**
* Average loser: **−1.1%**

Such a strategy can outperform one with a much higher win rate but poor risk/reward.

# What limits prediction?

Financial markets are **complex adaptive systems**.

Their behavior changes because participants react to information and to each other.

Sources of uncertainty include:

* Unexpected geopolitical events
* Weather shocks
* Policy changes
* New regulations
* Technological disruptions
* Human psychology
* Large institutional orders
* Random market noise

No AI can perfectly anticipate all of these.

# What success looks like

A mature AI trading platform typically measures:

| Metric                              |                                      Good |
| ----------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------: |
| Directional accuracy                |                                    55–65% |
| Precision on high-confidence trades |                                    70–90% |
| Profit factor                       |                                      >1.5 |
| Sharpe ratio                        | >2 (excellent strategies may exceed this) |
| Maximum drawdown                    |   As low as practical relative to returns |
| Positive expectancy                 |                                 Essential |

Notice that **directional accuracy is not the primary metric**.

# What determines success more than the AI model?

In many institutional settings, the model is only one component.

A useful rule of thumb is:

* **Data quality:** ~40%
* **Feature engineering:** ~25%
* **Model selection and training:** ~15%
* **Risk management:** ~15%
* **Execution quality:** ~5%

These percentages are illustrative rather than universal, but they reflect a widely held view in quantitative finance: **better data and better features often matter more than a more sophisticated algorithm.**

# Can it become autonomous?

Yes, but autonomy exists on a spectrum.

A highly autonomous trading system can:

* Collect data automatically.
* Validate and clean data.
* Engineer features.
* Detect market regimes.
* Forecast probabilities.
* Decide whether to trade.
* Size positions.
* Execute trades.
* Monitor risk.
* Learn from outcomes.

Even then, institutional firms usually keep **human oversight**, especially for exceptional events, changing regulations, infrastructure failures, or situations outside the model's experience.

## A realistic expectation

If you build a system that:

* Uses diverse, high-quality data,
* Continuously updates its understanding of the market,
* Detects changing regimes,
* Estimates probabilities rather than certainties,
* Applies disciplined risk management,
* And learns from new information,

you can build a system that is **consistently profitable over the long term**.

However, no system can **perfectly predict** future market movements. Financial markets are inherently uncertain, and even the most advanced quantitative firms focus on maintaining a **statistical edge** rather than eliminating uncertainty.
